I edit video in DaVinci Resolve, and I write code. VidToFLAC exists because, editing my own footage, I ran into a failure nothing explained properly: you import a clip, the picture comes in fine and the sound does not. No clear error, no hint, just a grey bar where a waveform should be.

It took me a while to find the cause — the audio codec, not the file and not the project — and considerably longer to find a fix that did not involve uploading my video to someone else's server. So I wrote the tool I needed, and then the guides I would have liked to find. This page is about who is behind those texts and, above all, how what they say gets checked.

What I write about here

The guides cover one very specific problem and what surrounds it: why a video editor ends up with no sound, which codec each format carries, when the video can be copied without re-encoding and when it cannot, and which FFmpeg commands actually do what they promise. I do not write about what I have not tested.

What I test it on

Everything published here is checked on this machine:

  • DaVinci Resolve Studio 21 on Ubuntu, which is where the audio problem shows up in its clearest form.
  • FFmpeg installed on the system, to build the test files and to run the commands before recommending them.
  • Real files rather than descriptions of files: screen recordings, camera and phone footage, and files created on purpose with whichever codec needs measuring.

How I verify what I publish

The method is simple, and it explains why some statements on this site contradict others elsewhere:

  • Commands are run, not reasoned about. If a guide recommends an FFmpeg line, that line has been run against a test file and the result inspected with ffprobe.
  • The text is checked against the tool's own code, not against what another article claims. If the converter re-encodes a codec, the guide says so; if it copies it, it says that instead.
  • What turns out to be wrong gets corrected and reported. When a test contradicts something already published, the text changes, however awkward that is.

What I have corrected after testing it

Three real examples from this site, each from a test that came out the opposite of what was expected:

  • AAC in DaVinci Resolve Studio on Linux. Several guides — mine among them — took it for granted that the paid edition solves the problem. I recorded the test: an MP4 with AAC-LC audio, the most common profile, imported into Studio 21 on Linux, came in with no sound. You can watch the recording on the home page. The site's tables and FAQs have been corrected.
  • The command that did not fix variable frame rate. Three guides recommended -vsync cfr -c:v copy to go from VFR to CFR. Measured with ffprobe, the output kept the original frame rate: copying the stream gives no way to rescale frame timestamps. The guides now explain the approach that does work.
  • "Every audio track is converted." Three guides promised it, and it matters most to anyone recording game audio and a microphone on separate tracks in OBS. Tested with a two-track file, one track came out, and the text was changed to say so plainly. Then the tool changed: since 24 September 2026 it detects the tracks and lets you choose which to keep. Before rewriting the guides I tested it with a two-track file — both came out, in MKV and in MP4 — and only then changed the text.
  • "Resolve shows you the tracks when you import the clip." I wrote that myself in the OBS guide: that the Inspector displayed a selector listing the available tracks. Dragging in an MKV holding two FLAC tracks — confirmed with ffprobe — Resolve Studio 21.0.4 imported it with a single one. I generated thirteen variants changing one property at a time and none of them explained the difference. The guide now says what actually happens, and how to get both tracks out through Clip Attributes.
