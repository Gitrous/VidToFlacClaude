Why DaVinci Resolve Free often struggles with H.265

H.265 — also called HEVC, High Efficiency Video Coding — is the video compression standard that succeeds H.264. It delivers the same visual quality at roughly half the file size, which is why modern phones and cameras favour it for 4K recording.

HEVC support is not a yes-or-no matter: it depends on the DaVinci Resolve version, the operating system and the machine's hardware decoding capabilities. The same file can open fine on one computer and fail on another.

The root of it is that decoding H.265 requires a patent licence, and Blackmagic Design applies different restrictions depending on which DaVinci Resolve you have:

Free DaVinci Resolve: H.265 decoding depends entirely on the GPU. If your graphics card has no hardware HEVC decoder — many older or integrated GPUs do not — DaVinci Resolve Free cannot open the file. On Linux, H.265 support in the free version is all but non-existent.

DaVinci Resolve Studio: includes software H.265 decoding, so it works on any GPU.

The most common symptoms are:

  • The clip appears in the Media Pool with a red X, or the thumbnail never loads
  • On import, DaVinci Resolve shows the error Unsupported video codec
  • The clip imports but plays back as black
  • DaVinci Resolve slows to a crawl or hangs when opening high-resolution H.265 files

Alternativa con FFmpeg en la terminal

If you have FFmpeg installed, this command converts H.265 to H.264 at high quality:

ffmpeg -i video_hevc.mp4 -c:v libx264 -crf 18 -preset ultrafast -c:a flac output.mkv

To process a whole folder of H.265 files:

for f in *.mp4; do ffmpeg -i "$f" -c:v libx264 -crf 18 -preset ultrafast -c:a flac "${f%.mp4}.mkv"; done

If you would rather favour quality over speed — slower, but a smaller H.264 file — use -preset slow instead of -preset ultrafast.

How to avoid the problem in future

If you want your recordings to arrive already compatible with DaVinci Resolve Free, with no intermediate step, you have two options:

Option A: record in H.264 from the start

Most cameras and phones let you choose the video codec:

  • iPhone: Settings → Camera → Formats → Most Compatible, which records H.264
  • Android: camera app settings → Video quality → look for the H.264 or "compatible" option
  • DJI: in the DJI Fly app → Video settings → Codec → H.264
  • Sony/Canon/Fuji mirrorless: Menu → Recording format → XAVC S (H.264) instead of XAVC HS (H.265)

The trade-off is that H.264 files at 4K are significantly larger than H.265 ones.

Option B: use proxies in DaVinci Resolve

If you have DaVinci Resolve Studio or a compatible GPU, you can generate proxies — lightweight H.264 versions of the clips to edit with — while keeping the original H.265 files for the final render. In DaVinci Resolve: right-click the clip → Generate Proxy Media.

Frequently asked questions

Why can't free DaVinci Resolve import H.265?

DaVinci Resolve Free relies on the GPU to decode H.265. If your GPU has no hardware HEVC decoder, or if you are on Linux, decoding fails. DaVinci Resolve Studio includes a software decoder that works on any system. The quickest fix is to convert the H.265 to H.264 with VidToFLAC before importing.

Does H.265 work in DaVinci Resolve Studio but not in Free?

Yes. DaVinci Resolve Studio includes software H.265 decoding, which works regardless of the GPU. The free version can only use hardware GPU decoding, which is not available on every setup. That is why the same file can work in Studio and not in Free.

Do you lose much quality converting H.265 to H.264?

At CRF 18, which is what VidToFLAC uses, the loss is visually imperceptible. The resulting file will be somewhat larger than the original H.265 because H.264 compresses less efficiently, but the visual quality is practically identical. For working material in an edit, the difference does not exist in practice.

My iPhone records in H.265 — how do I make that work with DaVinci Resolve?

Two options: (1) set the iPhone to record H.264 by going to Settings → Camera → Formats → Most Compatible; (2) convert the videos you have already shot with VidToFLAC before importing them into DaVinci Resolve. The first affects future recordings; the second sorts out the footage you already have.

How long does converting H.265 to H.264 take?

It depends on the clip's resolution and length, and on how powerful your computer is. Roughly, a one-minute 4K H.265 clip takes between 1 and 3 minutes with VidToFLAC using the ultrafast preset. For long material, consider FFmpeg in the terminal with GPU acceleration (-hwaccel cuda for NVIDIA, or -hwaccel vaapi on Linux).
