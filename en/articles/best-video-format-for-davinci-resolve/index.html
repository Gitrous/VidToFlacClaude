Choosing the right video format in DaVinci Resolve is the difference between a smooth edit and one full of stuttering, codec errors and audio problems. Not every format behaves the same in the editor: some are made for editing, others for distribution, and mixing them up causes no end of headaches. Container vs. codec: the most common confusion in DaVinci Resolve

Before getting to specific formats, one distinction needs clearing up, because it causes a great deal of confusion: the container and the codec are different things.

  • The container — the file format — is the box holding the video, audio and metadata tracks together: .mp4, .mov, .mkv, .mxf and so on.
  • The codec is the algorithm compressing the video or audio inside that box: H.264, H.265, ProRes, AAC, FLAC.

The same container can hold different codecs. An .mp4 can carry H.264 or H.265 video with AAC or MP3 audio. An .mkv can carry exactly the same codecs. What determines compatibility with DaVinci Resolve is the codec, not the container.

A concrete example: an .mkv with AAC audio will hit exactly the same problems in DaVinci Resolve on Linux as an .mp4 with AAC audio. Changing the extension from MP4 to MKV fixes nothing — the audio codec has to change.

Video codecs in DaVinci Resolve: the full comparison

DaVinci Resolve supports a wide range of video codecs, but they do not all behave the same while editing. There are two kinds: distribution codecs, heavily compressed and expensive to decode, and editing codecs, less compressed and far lighter to process in real time.

H.264: the most common, not the easiest to edit

H.264 is the most widespread video codec in the world: smartphones, action cameras, screen recorders and most YouTube video all use it. DaVinci Resolve supports it on every platform, but with one drawback: it is an inter-frame codec, meaning each frame depends on the frames before and after it to decode. That makes scrubbing the timeline slow and editing CPU-hungry.

For short projects, or on a powerful machine, H.264 is perfectly usable. For long projects or modest hardware, consider generating proxies — low-resolution copies — from the DaVinci Resolve menu.

H.265 (HEVC): better quality, heavier processing

H.265 delivers the same visual quality as H.264 in half the space, which is why modern 4K cameras use it. The catch is that decoding it in real time takes roughly twice the computing power. On Linux, support in the free version is limited. If your footage is H.265 and the edit stutters, generate proxies or transcode to DNxHR before editing.

ProRes: the professional standard on Mac

Apple ProRes (422, 422 HQ, 4444) is the editing codec of choice in Mac environments. It is intra-frame — every frame stands alone — which makes scrubbing the timeline instant. DaVinci Resolve supports it natively on Mac and Windows. On Linux, ProRes support is read-only and may need additional packages.

DNxHD / DNxHR: the ProRes of Windows and Linux

Developed by Avid, DNxHD (for HD) and DNxHR (for 2K/4K) are the cross-platform equivalent of ProRes. They work equally well on Windows, Mac and Linux, and DaVinci Resolve supports them without trouble. If you work on Linux or in a mixed environment, DNxHR is your best choice for editing media.

Audio codecs in DaVinci Resolve: which to use and which to avoid

Audio is where most import errors happen in DaVinci Resolve, especially on Linux. The practical recommendation: if you need lossless audio and want to avoid compatibility surprises, FLAC is the safest choice. AAC and MP3 work on Windows and macOS, but not on Linux.

FLAC versus WAV: both are lossless and both work perfectly in DaVinci Resolve. FLAC's advantage is that it compresses the audio by 40 to 60% without discarding any data. On long videos, that size difference is substantial. FLAC is the better choice unless you need maximum compatibility with very old software.

Which format to use in DaVinci Resolve for your camera or source

Smartphone (iPhone, Android)

Smartphones record .mp4 with H.264 or H.265 video and AAC audio. The video is perfectly usable in DaVinci Resolve; the AAC audio can cause trouble on Linux. The most practical fix: convert the MP4 to MKV with FLAC audio before importing. The video stays untouched and the audio works everywhere.

Mirrorless or DSLR camera (Sony, Canon, Fuji, Nikon)

These record .mp4 or .mov with H.264 or H.265 and AAC or PCM audio. PCM works fine; AAC can fail on Linux. If the camera offers ALL-I recording, where every frame is independent, use it: editing will be far smoother than with standard IPB recording. For the audio, convert the MOV to FLAC if you are on Linux.

Cinema camera (Blackmagic, RED, ARRI)

These cameras record native RAW formats (BRAW, R3D, ARRIRAW) or high-quality codecs (ProRes, DNxHD). DaVinci Resolve is specifically optimised for them — indeed, Blackmagic designed DaVinci Resolve to work with BRAW natively. There is nothing to convert; import directly.

Screen recording (OBS, Bandicam, QuickTime)

OBS can record MKV or MP4 with H.264/H.265 and AAC or Opus audio. Opus inside WebM is particularly troublesome in DaVinci Resolve. Set OBS to record MKV with FLAC audio, or, if you already have the files, convert the WebM to FLAC.

Video downloaded from the internet (YouTube and the rest)

YouTube video usually downloads as WebM with Opus audio, or MP4 with AAC. Either way the audio can fail in DaVinci Resolve on Linux. Convert the audio to FLAC before importing. See our guide to converting WebM to FLAC to do it in seconds.

The ideal format for editing in DaVinci Resolve

There is no single answer, but there is a practical recommendation for each context:

  • Professional production on Mac: ProRes 422 HQ in a MOV container with FLAC audio.
  • Professional production on Windows or Linux: DNxHR in an MXF or MOV container with FLAC audio.
  • YouTube and social media projects: MKV with H.264 video — or H.265 if your GPU is strong — and FLAC audio. It is the most compatible and practical combination.
  • Editing smartphone footage: remux the original MP4 to MKV, changing only the audio to FLAC. You keep the video's original quality without spending time transcoding.
Avoid editing Variable Frame Rate (VFR) files directly. Smartphone video shot in automatic mode is usually VFR: the frame rate varies with the scene. DaVinci Resolve can drift the audio out of sync with such files. Convert to Constant Frame Rate with FFmpeg before editing: ffmpeg -i input.mp4 -vsync cfr -c:v copy -c:a flac output.mkv

Which format to choose when exporting from DaVinci Resolve

Exporting is a different matter from editing: here you want compatibility and small files, not editing performance.

For YouTube or Vimeo

H.264 in MP4 with AAC audio is the universal standard. In DaVinci Resolve, use the "YouTube" or "Vimeo" preset on the Deliver page — they are tuned for those platforms. Recommended bitrate: 16–25 Mbps for 1080p, 35–45 Mbps for 4K.

For archival or master delivery

ProRes 4444 (Mac) or DNxHR 444 (every platform) with FLAC audio. The files are large, but they preserve all the colour and audio information for any future re-edit.

For social media (Instagram, TikTok)

H.264 in MP4, 1080p maximum — Instagram has been capping at 1080p even if you upload 4K, as of September 2026 — with AAC audio at 320 kbps. Most platforms recompress on upload, so there is no point exporting at excessively high quality.

For client delivery or post-production

Use whatever format the client or the next link in the production chain specifies. Absent any spec, ProRes 422 HQ or DNxHR HQ are the most widely accepted professional delivery formats.

Frequently asked questions about formats in DaVinci Resolve

Which video format is best for editing in DaVinci Resolve?

For professional editing, ProRes 422 (Mac) or DNxHR (Windows/Linux) are best: intra-frame, free of compression artefacts and excellent in real time. For home or YouTube projects, MKV with H.264 and FLAC audio is the most practical and compatible option.

Does DaVinci Resolve support H.265 (HEVC)?

Yes, on Windows and macOS. On Linux, support is limited in the free version. H.265 also needs considerably more CPU/GPU power than H.264 to decode in real time. If the edit stutters, generate proxies from Clip → Generate Optimized Media.

Can I edit MP4 files directly in DaVinci Resolve?

Yes. H.264 video inside an MP4 is supported on every platform. AAC audio may not play on Linux. For comfortable editing on long projects, consider generating proxies or remuxing to MKV with FLAC audio to sidestep codec problems.

Which audio codec should I use in DaVinci Resolve?

FLAC. It is the lossless audio codec DaVinci Resolve supports on every platform, Linux included, and it takes far less space than PCM/WAV without discarding any data. AAC works on Windows and macOS but not on Linux. E-AC-3 works on no platform at all.

MKV or MOV for DaVinci Resolve?

Both work well. MOV is preferable on Mac with ProRes; MKV is more open and the better choice on Linux or with FLAC audio. What actually matters is the codec inside, not the container.

What is Variable Frame Rate, and why does it cause problems in DaVinci Resolve?

Variable Frame Rate means the frame rate changes during recording — common in smartphone video shot in automatic mode. DaVinci Resolve can drift the audio out of sync with such files. The fix is to convert to Constant Frame Rate with FFmpeg before importing: ffmpeg -i input.mp4 -vsync cfr -c:v copy -c:a flac output.mkv
