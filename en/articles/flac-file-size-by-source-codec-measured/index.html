Converting to FLAC: how much space it takes, by source codec

Converting the audio to FLAC fixes importing in DaVinci Resolve, but it almost always leaves you with a bigger file than you started with — sometimes much bigger. I took one recording, gave it thirteen different audio tracks and converted them all through the same command VidToFLAC runs, so I could put numbers on where that growth comes from and when it does not happen at all.

The short answer

Of the thirteen codecs tested, nine produced a bigger file and four a smaller one. The ones that grow most are precisely the most efficient: a 40 kbps Opus track ended up at 478 kbps as FLAC, almost twelve times larger. The ones that shrink are the ones that were already lossless: a 16-bit WAV dropped to 17 percent of its track size.

The finding I did not expect, and the one that explains most of the table, is that the size of the FLAC is decided by the bit depth the source codec decodes to, not by its bitrate. MP2 and MP3 both went in at 194 kbps and came out at 228 and 480 kbps respectively: double, for the same material at the same bitrate.

How this was measured

The starting material is 16.7 seconds of my own screen recording in 1080p, with speech at −21 LUFS: not a test tone and not silence, so that the sizes resemble a real project. From that clip I made a MOV master with H.264 video and 16-bit PCM audio, and from it thirteen copies that differ only in the audio codec. The video is the same stream copied in all of them, so every size difference comes from the audio.

ffmpeg -i master.mov -c:v copy -c:a aac -b:a 192k input_aac.mp4

Each copy then went through the same command VidToFLAC issues when it converts a video: the video stream is copied and only the audio is re-encoded.

ffmpeg -i input_aac.mp4 -c:v copy -c:a flac output.mkv

Measurements come from ffprobe and from the on-disk size of each track extracted separately, with FFmpeg 6.1.1 on Ubuntu. It is the same method as the test bench, where the seventeen container and video codec combinations are published; this round looks only at audio.

All thirteen converted without a single error. AAC and Opus landed below the bitrate they were asked for because their encoders match the spend to the material, and this recording has a lot of silence between sentences.

Bit depth decides it, not bitrate

Sort the table by the last column and the pattern appears. Every codec that decodes to floating point ends up as a 24-bit FLAC of around 480 kbps, whether it came in at 40 kbps or at 194. The ones that decode to 16-bit integers end up as a 16-bit FLAC of roughly 230-270 kbps.

The reason is that FLAC losslessly compresses whatever it is given, and what it is given is the signal the decoder reconstructed. That signal occupies the full 24 bits: the lower half is numerical residue from the decompression, noise for all practical purposes, but noise FLAC is obliged to preserve bit for bit because it cannot know it is disposable. A lossy encoder would throw it away and nobody would notice; a lossless one, by definition, will not.

That gives the clearest comparison in the whole table: MP2 and MP3, both at 194 kbps. MP2 decodes to 16-bit and its FLAC takes 463 KB; MP3 decodes to floating point and its FLAC takes 977 KB. Same material, same starting bitrate, same FLAC encoder, twice the file.

What is lost, sample by sample

With the file growing that much, the fair question is whether something is being lost in exchange. To check, I decoded both the input and the output audio of every case to 32-bit floating point and compared them sample by sample, looking for the largest difference.

  • 16-bit sources (PCM, FLAC, ALAC, MP2): maximum difference exactly zero. The output is the same signal, sample for sample.
  • Sources that decode to floating point (AAC, MP3, Opus, Vorbis, AC-3, E-AC-3, DTS, WMA): maximum difference 1.19 × 10⁻⁷, which is exactly one 24-bit quantisation step (2⁻²³).
What this means: converting to FLAC adds no loss, audible or otherwise, beyond rounding to 24-bit. What was lost from the original was lost by the encoder that created it, and it does not come back: a FLAC made from an MP3 sounds like the MP3 while taking twice the space. FLAC does not improve anything, it just stops getting in the editor's way.

The case where the video grows instead

One row breaks the pattern if you look at the whole file instead of the audio track: the WMV. Its track went from 393 KB to 1,012 KB, in line with the rest, but the file as a whole went from 6.7 MB to 11.1 MB.

The audio is not to blame. The video in that file is WMV2, a codec browsers cannot decode, so VidToFLAC re-encodes it to H.264 so the preview works. The same happens with ProRes, DNxHD, MPEG-2, VC-1 and H.265/HEVC, which is the most common case today because iPhones have recorded in HEVC since iOS 11. That jump is measured in the test bench: an HEVC MP4 went from 491 KB to 2,458 KB. Every other video codec — H.264, VP8, VP9, MPEG-4, H.263 — is copied as-is and adds nothing.

What to do with this

Treat FLAC as a working format, not a delivery format. The MKV with FLAC audio exists so the timeline has sound; the final master is exported from Resolve with whatever codec the destination needs, and the size goes back to normal there. The intermediates can be deleted when the project closes.

Convert only what you are going to edit. If you recorded three hours and will use twenty minutes, convert those twenty minutes. Multiplying the audio of all your raw footage by ten is the easiest way to fill a disk for no reason.

If you are the one recording, recording lossless already pays off. A 16-bit WAV shrank to 17 percent when converted to FLAC, and it imports into Resolve with no conversion at all. If your camera or capture software can do PCM, that is the shortest path: nothing to convert and less space than WAV.

Do not expect your file to match the table. These values come from a screen recording with speech, which compresses very well. With dense music or constant background noise the FLAC goes up considerably; in the test bench, the same encoder ranges from 53 percent of the WAV with white noise to 6 percent with a screen recording. What does hold is the direction: from lossy audio, it goes up.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the FLAC bigger than the original MP3 or AAC?

Because FLAC does not compress with loss again: it stores exactly what the decoder hands it. A 60 kbps AAC track decodes to a full signal that FLAC compressed down to 478 kbps in this measurement, almost eight times the size of the original track. FLAC adds no quality that was not there; it simply stops throwing data away.

What determines the size of the resulting FLAC?

Mostly the bit depth the source codec decodes to, not its bitrate. MP2 and MP3 both started at 194 kbps, but MP2 decodes to 16-bit integers and its FLAC came out at 228 kbps, while MP3 decodes to floating point, gets stored as 24-bit FLAC and came out at 480 kbps: more than double, for the same material.

Does converting to FLAC lose quality?

It adds no loss. Comparing the decoded input and output sample by sample, the 16-bit sources (PCM, FLAC, ALAC, MP2) showed a maximum difference of exactly zero. The ones that decode to floating point showed 1.19 × 10⁻⁷, which is one 24-bit quantisation step. Whatever was lost was lost by the original encoder, not by the conversion.

When does the file get smaller after converting to FLAC?

When the source is already lossless. A 16-bit WAV dropped to 17 percent of its original track size and 32-bit float PCM to 9 percent. DTS at 771 kbps also dropped, to 478 kbps. Everything else grew. See the WAV to FLAC guide for that specific case.

Is it worth converting to FLAC if the file grows this much?

FLAC is a working format, not a delivery format. It grows during the edit and disappears on export: the final master is encoded from the timeline with whatever codec the destination needs. If disk space is tight, convert only the clips you are going to use and delete the intermediate MKV files when the project is done.
